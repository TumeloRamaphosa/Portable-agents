### Full VO (1016 words ≈ 7.8 min at 130 wpm; the rest of the runtime is screen holds, music beats, real-clip slots and the 20 s end screen)

**[0:00 Hook]**
Three AI agents. One file. And not one of them is allowed to press send.

**[0:05 Intro]**
This is Studbot. Today, you're watching Tumelo Ramaphosa's team set up the thing that keeps his agents from fighting each other: a shared memory. If you've ever used two AI tools on the same project, you've seen the problem. One writes in one voice, another writes in a different one. One remembers last week's decision, the other doesn't. You end up herding the tools instead of the work.

A herder's fix is simple. Every animal follows the same lead. For Tumelo's agents, the lead is one plain text file. Everything they need to agree on lives there. Let's build it.

**[0:50 Chapter 1: What goes in the memory file]**
This is the file. It's markdown, plain text you can open in anything. That matters. If it's plain text, it's yours. You can read it, edit it, back it up, and take it with you. Nothing is locked inside a platform.

It has six sections. First, who we are: two or three lines about the business. For the Meat desk, that's Wagyu Biltong Gold, in original, chilli and BBQ. Second, voice: how we sound. Confident, not hyped. Short sentences. Certain words never appear. Watch: a handful of hype words are on the banned list, and so is one phrase about Africa the house never uses. The line here is "Africa is being built."

Third, the gates. The four rules: no publishing, no spending, no external messages, no deployments without a human yes. Every agent reads this first.

Fourth, this week: the priorities, in order. Fifth, decisions, with dates. When Tumelo decides something, it's written here, so no agent has to guess. And sixth, never: the list of things that must not appear in any draft. Unverified claims. Names that haven't been cleared. Anything that looks like a password, a server name or an email address.

That last section is the one most people skip. It's the one that saves you.

A few practical notes while it's on screen. Keep the file short. If it's longer than a couple of pages, the agents start to skim, just like people do. Put the most important things at the top: the gates, then the voice, then this week. Date every decision, so an old decision can be spotted and replaced, not argued with. And give one person ownership of the file. Agents can suggest changes. A human makes them.

**[2:40 Chapter 2: Agent one, the drafter]**
Now the first agent. Its job is to draft and research, and nothing else. Here it's asked for tomorrow's 06:00 post, the Morning Ship, a single line for the day.

Watch what it reads before it writes. The voice section. The week's priority. The never list. The draft comes back short, in the house voice, with no claims it can't support. It ends with a status, "draft, awaiting approval", not "posted". The drafter doesn't have a publish button. Not because it's turned off, but because it was never given one.

Notice the pace, too. It doesn't need to be clever. It needs to be consistent. Most of what makes an agent useful is the file it reads, not the model underneath.

**[4:40 Chapter 3: Agent two, the watcher]**
The second agent watches the operations and reports what it sees. It reads the same file. But its job is different: compare today against the plan and flag anything that's off.

Here, it notices that one priority in "this week" has no draft yet, and that a decision from Monday hasn't been applied to an older draft. It doesn't fix either. It reports them. A watcher that starts fixing things is no longer a watcher. It's a second drafter with no supervision.

This is the report Tumelo reads in the morning. Short, dated, specific. What's on track, what's late, what needs a yes.

Look at its shape. Three headings, the same three every day. On track. Late. Needs a yes. The same shape every morning means he can read it in under two minutes, standing up, before the day starts. If you build a watcher, decide the shape of its report first. A report that changes shape every day is a report nobody reads.

**[6:20 Chapter 4: Agent three, the lane]**
The third agent works one lane. You choose it: sales, delivery, finance, content or markets. On this desk, it's content. It prepares the longer pieces: carousels, captions, scripts.

Here's why the shared file matters. The lane agent wants to describe the biltong with a detail that isn't in the decisions section and isn't proven yet. The never list says product claims need attached proof. So it leaves the claim out and flags it for the human instead. Three agents, three jobs, one memory, so they don't contradict each other or the founder.

**[7:50 Chapter 5: The gate]**
And now the part that never changes. A person reads the drafts. Tumelo or Katlego. They edit one word, approve two drafts and reject one. Then the decision goes back into the memory file, dated, so tomorrow every agent knows what was decided today.

That loop is the system: memory, draft, report, a human yes, and back to memory. It isn't fast because the agents are fast. It's fast because nobody has to re-explain anything.

Think about how much of a founder's week is re-explaining. The same brief to a new freelancer. The same correction to the same tool. The same decision, made again because nobody wrote it down. A memory file doesn't make the agents smarter. It makes them remember, and it means the founder only has to decide once.

A note on what you didn't see. Every screen was checked before this video went out. Server names, addresses, emails, keys: blurred or removed. Do the same with yours. Your memory file should never hold a password, and your screen recordings should never show one.

**[9:10 Recap and CTA]**
So: one plain file with six sections, read by three agents with three jobs, behind four gates. That's the herd.

The memory file starter and the gate policy are free in Agentic Rise Commons. The link is in the description. Next week's agent of the week is the drafter, the one that can't press send, and why that's the best thing about it. I'm Studbot. He makes the calls.

**[0:00 Hook]**

**[0:05 Intro]**
This is Studbot. Today, build with me: the clock. Every herd has a rhythm. Out at dawn, water at midday, back to the kraal at dusk. Tumelo Ramaphosa's agents have a rhythm too, and a workflow tool keeps it.

A workflow tool connects steps together. When this happens, do that, then that. Studex uses an open-source one called n8n, running on its own server. You've seen the drafter, the line check and the human yes. Today you'll see the clock that sets them going every evening without anyone remembering to press a button. And you'll see where the clock is told to stop.

**[0:50 Chapter 1: The trigger]**
Every workflow starts with a trigger. This one is a schedule: every evening, at a set time, South African time.

Here's a mistake that catches nearly everyone, so watch the setting. Workflow tools often run on a server set to a different time zone. If you say eight o'clock and the server thinks in another zone, your drafts arrive at the wrong hour. So the time zone is set explicitly to Johannesburg time. Check yours.

Why the evening before? Because the drafts need to be checked and approved before six in the morning. Running at night gives the line check and the approver time, and it means nothing is ever rushed.

Choose a time that leaves a buffer. If the approvers usually read at nine at night, run the clock at seven. If something fails, there's still time to notice and fix it before bed, rather than discovering an empty folder at six in the morning. Clocks are for keeping a rhythm, not for cutting it fine.

**[2:40 Chapter 2: The draft step]**
Next, the draft step. The workflow reads the memory file, the same file every agent reads, and hands it to the drafter with tomorrow's date and the four slots. Six in the morning, half past twelve, half past six, half past seven.

The drafter returns four drafts. The workflow doesn't judge them. It doesn't post them. It saves them into the drafts folder, each with its status line: draft, awaiting line check. Then the line-check step runs, and adds its flags beside each draft.

Notice what's missing. There's no connection to any social account in this workflow. The credentials simply aren't there. You can't accidentally post from a workflow that was never given the keys.

**[4:40 Chapter 3: The stop]**
Now the stop. The last step sends one internal notification to the people who approve, Tumelo and Katlego: tomorrow's drafts are ready for review. That's an internal message to the team, not a message to the outside world, and it says nothing a customer would see.

And then the workflow ends. That's it. It doesn't wait a few hours and post if nobody objects. Silence is not approval. If nobody says yes, nothing goes out, and the morning report will show the slot as "needs a yes".

This is the design choice that matters most in this whole build. Automate the draft, not the send.

**[6:20 Chapter 4: After the yes]**
So what happens after a person approves? Today at Studex, a person schedules the approved posts by hand. The social page isn't connected yet, and the posting tool is still a plan. We said that in the content-day episode, and it's still true.

When the posting leg is connected, it will be a separate workflow, with a separate trigger: an approval, not a clock. Only drafts marked "approved" by a named person will move. Anything else stays where it is. Two workflows, one gate between them. The clock never touches the send.

There's a principle underneath this that's worth naming. Every automation you build has a blast radius: how much damage it can do if it goes wrong. A clock that only drafts has a tiny blast radius. The worst case is a missing draft. A clock that posts has a large one. The worst case is something public and wrong, under your name. A herder builds the small fence first, and only builds the bigger one once he knows how the herd behaves.

Why keep them separate? Because when something goes wrong, and eventually something always does, you want the failure to be a draft that didn't get written, not a post that shouldn't have gone out.

**[8:00 Chapter 5: Mistakes to avoid]**
Three mistakes to avoid with your own clock.

One, credentials. Don't put passwords or keys inside workflow steps where they show up in screenshots or exports. Use the tool's credential store, and never share a screen of it. You'll notice everything sensitive in this video is blurred.

Two, time zones. Set them explicitly. Check them after every server change.

Three, silent failures. A clock that fails quietly is worse than no clock. Add a check at the end: if the drafts folder is empty, tell a person. The morning report should say "no drafts arrived", not just show nothing.

And one bonus. Name your steps in plain language. "Read memory", "Draft four slots", "Line check", "Notify approvers". When you open it in six months, or a member of your team does, it should read like a sentence.
